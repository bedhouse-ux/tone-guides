Sources: Buckley's primary guitar throughout his career was a borrowed 1983 Fender Telecaster (top loader). He biamped live: Fender Vibroverb '63 RI for clean tones, Mesa Boogie Dual Rectifier Trem-O-Verb for overdrive. The Alesis Quadraverb rack unit was his primary and almost only effect — Hallelujah used a modified version of its "Taj Mahal" preset. The reverb on Grace is long-decay hall character with modulation — notes linger, stack, and create tension. Buckley used very few pedals; his tone was almost entirely guitar, amp, and the Quadraverb. Additional tools: DigiTech Whammy II, Boss Hyper Fuzz, EHX Hot Tubes, Marshall Drivemaster. He played Dean Markley Blue Steel strings.
Tone Character

The Two Sides

Grace is a record of two emotional extremes: clear, articulate clean tones soaked in hall reverb (Hallelujah, Lover, Lilac Wine) and explosive, violent overdrive that breaks without warning (Mojo Pin, Eternal Life). Both sides are radical. The clean sounds impossibly pure; the dirt sounds genuinely dangerous.

The Reverb Doctrine

The Quadraverb hall reverb is not decoration — it is compositional. Buckley deliberately let notes overlap and dissonances linger. The reverb creates harmonic tension he never fully resolves. The decay should be long enough that each chord you play is still audible when the next one arrives. Notes stack, blur, and breathe.

Hall Reverb · Quadraverb Settings
The Most Important Setting In This Guide
The Alesis Quadraverb "Taj Mahal" preset is a large hall reverb with significant diffusion and very long decay — Buckley modified it slightly but kept the core character. This reverb is not a background effect. It is compositional. Notes must linger long enough to overlap with subsequent notes, creating internal dissonance that never fully resolves. The hall should feel like a cathedral, not a room.

The reverb philosophy: Set the decay so long that when you play a chord, the previous chord is still audible. This is deliberate — Buckley was composing with the reverb. On Hallelujah, the long decay under the melody is what creates the aching, unresolved quality. If the reverb isn't making individual notes feel like they're dissolving into each other, it's not long enough. Push the decay past your comfort zone.

Slapback Delay
Short · Tight · Adds Thickness
Short slapback (~80–120ms) on overdrive adds thickness and depth without washing. Very low mix (10–15%). The overdrive rig is much drier than the clean rig.

REVERB
Medium Hall
Shorter Decay Than Clean Rig
The overdrive rig uses a shorter reverb decay (1.5–2.5 sec) — the gain and sustain carry the tone, the reverb just provides air. Still hall character, but far more restrained than the clean rig.
ALWAYS ON
Overdrive rig philosophy: The Mesa Trem-O-Verb is aggressive — it does most of the tonal work. Don't over-effect the overdrive rig. Buckley's Eternal Life and Mojo Pin gain is primarily the amp, with the fuzz added only at peak moments. The reverb is shorter to let the distortion character breathe. If the overdrive rig sounds too wet or washy, pull the reverb mix back further.

Playing Notes
Technique
Dynamics · Volume Knob · Chord Voicings
Buckley's tone was almost entirely in his hands. His guitar voicings — heavy use of 6th chords, suspended intervals, open strings against fretted notes — created harmonic tension the reverb then sustained. The guitar volume knob was a primary expressive tool.
Dynamics
Extreme Range
From barely-there whispers to violent attack — the full range in one song
Guitar Vol Knob
Use It
Roll back before a note, pick, then open it — violin-style swell attack
Chord Voicings
6ths + Suspensions
Buckley favoured 6th chords (1-3-5-6) and suspended voicings — emotionally ambiguous
String Gauge
.010s
Dean Markley Blue Steel — light gauge for bending expressiveness
Let Notes Decay
Don't Hurry
With long reverb, pauses are as expressive as the notes. Silence into the reverb tail has texture.
Vibrato
Wide + Slow
Buckley's vibrato was vocal — wide pitch movement, deliberately paced
The reverb is not separate from the playing: Buckley composed with the reverb as a harmonic instrument. He would play a dissonant note knowing it would hang under the next chord, creating temporary tension. If you treat the reverb as background decoration and play as though it isn't there, you'll miss the entire technique. Slow down, let the reverb breathe, play into the decay.
